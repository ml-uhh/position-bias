A Transformer can give different influence to the same information depending on where it appears.

When information in the middle is underused compared with information near the beginning or end, this is often called Lost in the Middle.

A language model should useall the relevant informationwherever it appears in the context.

An illustration of position bias: information in the middle receives less influence.

The mechanism

The architectural origins.

Part of that imbalance can originate in the architecture itself.

Across layers

Every layer changes the balance.

Causal attention only looks backward. Repeating this transport across layers creates a pull toward earlier tokens, or primacy. Residual connections preserve the current representation, counteracting that pull. Positional encoding changes which earlier positions are favored. The balance between these mechanisms can produce broad profiles with both primacy and recency peaks.

We trace influence through successive layers by combining the paths through attention and residual connections. This is called attention rollout. We test it with ALiBi and RoPE, two common ways Transformers encode token position.

The mixing schedule

Residuals matter. Their strength matters too.

An attention-only rollout sends everything through attention. A fixed residual rollout divides the weight equally between attention and preservation. We instead measure their relative strength at each layer from forward activations.

R(t) = (1 − λt) I + λt A(t)

Preserve the current position + transport influence through attention

Here, I preserves position and A(t) transports influence through attention. The coefficient λt measures the attention branch’s share, accounting for reinforcement and cancellation between the two branches. We estimate the schedule independently of the influence curves used to evaluate the prediction. No rollout parameter is fitted to those curves.

The evidence

A structural explanation, tested against real models.

For ALiBi, we remove the content-dependent attention scores and retain the causal mask and positional slopes. For RoPE, position and content are intertwined. We therefore average token contributions across the corpus, accounting for the value and output projections that determine what attention writes into the model’s internal representation. Both constructions use the same residual mixing framework.

Across ten models and three corpora, adaptive residual mixing reduces the mean Wasserstein distance to measured influence from 0.62 for attention-only rollout and 0.60 for fixed residual mixing to 0.09, approximately an 85% reduction relative to either baseline.

Position bias does not require a learned preference for specific content. The architecture supplies a structural prior, and measuring the attention–residual balance makes its prediction much closer to observed influence. Although we measure token influence rather than downstream task accuracy, the profiles show the same broad pattern observed in Lost-in-the-Middle evaluations: stronger influence near the beginning and end, with weaker influence in the middle.
